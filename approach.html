The Approach

Most companies don’t have a marketing problem. They have a clarity problem.

Here’s how I think about it — and how I fix it.

Part one — The Clarity Gap

You can’t read the label from inside the jar.

If the gap is so visible to the market, why can’t leadership see it? Because you’re inside the jar. You’ve spent years fluent in your own language — your roadmap, your acronyms, your internal debates — and somewhere along the way you started talking to yourself instead of the market.

The Clarity Gap is what opens when a company can no longer tell one story about itself — when sales, marketing, product, and leadership each describe the business a little differently, and the market feels every inch of the difference.

When a company is small, clarity is free. The founder talks to customers every day. Product decisions happen in the same room as customer complaints. There aren’t enough layers for the story to drift. Then you succeed, and you specialize. Marketing, Sales, Product, and Customer Success each grow expert in their own view. Every one of those views is right. None of them is complete. And your customer doesn’t experience your departments — they experience one company.

Checkers vs. chess. I’ve always described it as the difference between checkers and chess. An expert plays their own square — four squares at most — and plays them well. The CEO has to see the whole board, because every move changes every other move. The higher you sit, the more of the board you see and the less of the detail. That’s not a flaw. That’s math. It’s also exactly how blind spots form.

You can usually feel a Clarity Gap before you can name it. A few tells:

  • Ten people in your company describe the business ten ways.
  • Your homepage sounds like your internal planning meeting, not your customer.
  • You’ve lost the one clean sentence that answers “what do you do?”
  • The market’s picture of you is the company from two years ago.

A Clarity Gap isn’t a failure of effort or intelligence. It’s the natural cost of growth. Healthy companies don’t avoid it — they just refuse to ignore it.

Part two — Go-to-Market Alignment

The structured way I close the gap.

Once you can see the gap, closing it is disciplined work, not inspiration. Every engagement runs through the same six-part progression. The scope changes. The method doesn’t.

  1. Market Definition. What market are we actually in? The market you’re best positioned to serve — not the one you inherited or the one competitors claim.
  2. Market Need. What do customers really hire us to solve? The problem in their words, and the forces driving them to act.
  3. Brand Definition. What should we stand for? What the company represents in the buyer’s mind, and the role it plays.
  4. Strategic Positioning. Why us? Why we win, and why anyone should believe it.
  5. Organizational Alignment. Does every team reinforce the same answer? Leadership, product, marketing, sales, customer success, and AI, all working from one playbook.
  6. Market Execution. Does it show up everywhere customers meet us? Website, sales narrative, content, thought leadership, AI — every touchpoint.

Do this, and the arguments get shorter, the decisions get faster, and the market finally meets the company you actually built.
