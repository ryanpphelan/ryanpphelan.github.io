When Alignment Becomes a Blindfold

Ryan Phelan

When a company’s growth stalls, the reflex is to buy more growth. I have spent most of my career arguing that the thing that stalled is usually not growth. It is clarity. I have described that clarity problem as having two sides, and I use a simple five-minute test to find which side you are on.

Two sides, one test

Inside, ask ten people what makes you different and why a buyer should choose you. If you get ten different answers, your gap is on the inside. Your own people are not aligned on the story.

Outside, ask ten customers and prospects what you actually do. If they describe the company you used to be, or the one next door, your gap is on the outside. The market has not caught up to what you are now.

The result the test misses

Then Trevor Hatfield, the CEO of SendWorks, left a comment on a LinkedIn post of mine that stopped me. He pointed out a result the test does not catch. Sometimes the ten inside all agree, say it crisply, and they are all wrong. Alignment passes with flying colors. Everyone tells the same story about what makes the company special. And it is a story the market does not buy on.

Alignment is not correctness

Here is what makes that dangerous. The inside test measures whether your people agree. It does not measure whether they are right. A leadership team can be perfectly aligned on a strength the market does not weigh, and the test still comes back green. In that moment, alignment stops being a green light. It becomes a blindfold. And the tighter and more confident the alignment, the thicker the blindfold, because conviction is the very thing that keeps anyone from questioning it.

So this is not a third gap. It is the outside gap in its most dangerous form, hidden behind inside success. The market is not confused about you. It understands you fine. It simply is not moved by the thing you are all proud of. You have not failed to tell your story. You have told it beautifully, and scaled it, and it is the wrong story.

Why you cannot see it from inside

It hides better than either clean case, because both of your tests pass. The inside ten agree. The outside ten can describe you. Every internal review comes back clean, and you are still losing, and nothing in the room will tell you why. You cannot find this one from inside, because the people who would have to spot it are the same people who are aligned around it. That is the whole reason it takes an outside read. Not because the people inside are not smart. They are usually smarter about the business than any outsider will ever be. It is because you cannot see the gap you are standing in, and agreement is what makes the gap invisible.

The third question

If you want to catch it, the two questions are not enough, because both come back clean. Add a third, aimed at the market’s decision rather than its comprehension: when you chose us, or chose someone else, what actually decided it? Then hold that answer against what your own people are proudest of. If the two do not line up, your alignment is hiding a problem, not solving one.

The fix has to come from outside the room

The fix is harder than either clean case, and it is worth being honest about that. You cannot close it by getting people to agree, because they already agree. You cannot close it by helping the market understand, because it already understands. You close it by moving a confident, aligned organization off the thing it is proud of and onto the thing the buyer actually decides on. You cannot do that by declaring the team wrong. Someone with the standing to be believed has to make the case for the better answer, out loud, with the rationale. That is leadership, and it almost always has to come from outside the consensus, because everyone inside it is holding the same blindfold.

Clarity is the price of admission

None of this makes clarity less important. You cannot even see this trap until you have closed the other two gaps, because a company that contradicts itself or gets described wrong has too much noise to read. Clarity is the price of admission. But passing both halves of the test does not mean you are safe. Sometimes it means you have hidden the problem better than anyone else could. Agreement is not the same as being right.

My thanks to Trevor Hatfield for the sharpest comment I have gotten in a while. The best frameworks get better in public.

If your growth has stalled and the inside story still sounds right to everyone in the room, that is the moment an outside read earns its keep. That is the work I do. Start a conversation.
